# A Portfolio Approach to Impact Investment (J.P. Morgan, 2012)

## What it is

A practical guide for institutional investors on building, analyzing, and managing portfolios of impact investments. The central contribution is a **three-dimensional portfolio framework** that adds "impact" as a third axis alongside the traditional return/risk axes of modern portfolio theory (MPT).

The research is grounded in J.P. Morgan Social Finance's own investment experience plus structured interviews with **23 institutional investors** (26 organizations total) spanning foundations, pension funds, financial institutions, and fund managers.

---

## Core Framework: The Three-Dimensional Portfolio Graph

Traditional MPT uses two parameters — risk and return — represented graphically. This paper extends it with **impact** as a third dimension.

Each investment is plotted as a **triangle** with vertices at:
- **Impact** — depth/breadth of social or environmental effect
- **Return** — financial return expectations
- **Risk** — financial and impact risk profile

The framework workflow:
1. **Map target profile** — define the shaded "target zone" (a range, not a point) that reflects the portfolio mandate
2. **Map individual investments** — each opportunity gets its own triangle; doesn't need to fit inside targets so long as the aggregate does
3. **Aggregate & compare** — overlay or notional-weighted-average individual triangles into one aggregate triangle; compare to target zone
4. **Expand dimensions (optional)** — split each axis into sub-components for richer analysis (e.g., Return → Income + Appreciation; Risk → Products + Process; Impact → Ecosystem + Investment) creating a six-pointed hexagon

Key design note from the paper: *"We imply no particular correlation or relationship between these three parameters."*

## Building an Impact Portfolio

### Organizational structures
| Structure | Description | Examples |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Separate team | Dedicated impact team alongside traditional/grant teams | J.P. Morgan Social Finance, Rockefeller Foundation |
| Hub-spoke | Central impact team + individual portfolio managers by instrument | PGGM, TIAA-CREF |
| Whole institution | Entire firm dedicated to impact | MicroVest, Bridges Ventures, F.B. Heron Foundation |

### Impact thesis components (Table 1)
| Target population | Target business model | Target impact |
|---|---|---|
| Income level | Product/service provider to target population | Number of target population reached |
| Degree of inclusion | Utilizing target population retail distribution | % of business reaching target population |
| Region of inhabitance | Utilizing target population suppliers | Scale/quality of outputs |
| — | Implementing energy and resource efficiency | — |

### Financial parameters
Investors set: eligible instruments, geographic/sector focus, growth stage and scalability targets, risk appetite.

**Key stance**: Abandon the return/impact trade-off debate. Instead, do **deal-by-deal economic analysis** of revenue potential and cost profile — do not make asset-class-level generalizations about whether impact requires sacrificing return.

## Risk Management

### Standard risks (same as traditional investing in same sector/region)
- Assessed on deal-by-deal basis, not as asset-class generalizations

### Cross-market risks unique to impact investing
1. **Early-stage ecosystem** — market infrastructure is immature; will ease over time
2. **Mission drift** — investee drifts from social mission as it scales
3. **Capital blending** — responsible combination of grants and investment capital
4. **Moral hazard** — disincentive to recognize impact failure or financial loss

### Risk management tools used by investors
- **Structural features**: seniority in capital structure, fund intermediaries, covenants referencing mission, compensation-linked incentives
- **Currency risk**: diversification across countries preferred over hedging
- **Mission alignment**: covenants that tie mission maintenance into deal terms

### Diversification approach
Impact investors take an **opportunistic** approach — monitor concentrations across sector, geography, instrument, and impact pursuit. More strategic once portfolio reaches scale.

## Looking Forward (2012 perspective)

Challenges expected to ease as market matures:
- Better benchmarking tools
- More standardized impact measurement
- Reduced ecosystem risks
- More deal flow reducing reliance on patient capital

**Tone**: Explicitly collaborative and field-building. Investors interviewed share information and best practices — not purely competitive.
