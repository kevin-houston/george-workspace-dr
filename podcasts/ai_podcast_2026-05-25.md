Two of the most consequential technology companies ever built are heading to the public markets within months of each other.

## SEGMENT 2 — UNCLE SAM WANTS TO SEE YOUR MODEL FIRST

**JORDAN:** On May 5th, the Center for AI Standards and Innovation — CAISI, which sits inside NIST at the Department of Commerce — announced it had signed pre-deployment evaluation agreements with Google DeepMind, Microsoft, and xAI. Those three join OpenAI and Anthropic, who signed earlier. Five labs, all of them now committed to government review before models ship.

**ALEX:** To understand why this matters, you have to understand what CAISI actually is. It's not a regulator with enforcement power. It's a research and standards body. These agreements are structured as Cooperative Research and Development Agreements — CRADAs — which means the government and the labs are technically collaborating, and proprietary information is protected.

**JORDAN:** But what happens under these agreements is consequential. Government evaluators — from agencies across the national security apparatus, organized through something called the TRAINS Taskforce — can assess frontier models before they're released to the public. The focus areas are cybersecurity risks, biosecurity risks, and chemical weapons risks.

**ALEX:** CAISI has now completed more than 40 assessments across the five labs. And there's a detail in the HPCwire and Axios reporting that I think gets undersold: this happened without legislation. No AI safety law was passed. No executive order mandated it. These are voluntary agreements that every major lab signed.

**JORDAN:** Which is a different story than what people expected twelve months ago. The dominant narrative was that the labs would resist regulation and the government was too slow to keep up. What's actually happened is that the labs — at least on this particular question — decided that a structured government relationship on pre-deployment safety was preferable to operating in ambiguity.

**ALEX:** That's a rational calculation. If you're deploying a model that could theoretically be used to help someone synthesize a dangerous pathogen, having a government evaluation that you passed is a legal and reputational shield.

**JORDAN:** There's also a competitive dimension. If you're a new entrant trying to build a frontier model, you now have to factor in whether you can handle a government evaluation process. That's a meaningful barrier.

**ALEX:** The open question is what happens when an evaluation finds something. The agreements don't include public disclosure requirements. We don't know if models have been delayed or modified based on CAISI findings. That's a genuine transparency gap.

**JORDAN:** Worth watching. Next up: the European Union agreed to make its landmark AI Act easier to comply with — and the details matter a lot more than the headline.

## SEGMENT 3 — THE EU BLINKS: AI ACT OMNIBUS VII

**ALEX:** On May 7th, the Council of the EU and the European Parliament reached a provisional agreement on what's being called the Digital Omnibus VII package, which amends the AI Act in several significant ways. The Council's press release is direct about the goal: simplify compliance, particularly for smaller companies.

**JORDAN:** Let's talk about what actually changed, because this is one of those stories where the details are everything.

The biggest change is timeline. Under the original AI Act, high-risk AI systems — that covers things like biometric identification, critical infrastructure management, education, employment, law enforcement, and border management — were required to comply with the full set of rules starting December 2026. Under the new agreement, that date moves to December 2027 for most high-risk use cases, and August 2028 for safety-component systems.

**ALEX:** So roughly a year to eighteen months of additional runway for organizations deploying AI in regulated contexts. That's significant if you're a hospital system, a bank, or a municipality trying to build out AI infrastructure.

**JORDAN:** The second major change: the definition of small and medium enterprises that get simplified compliance requirements now extends to companies with up to 500 employees. That's up from the original 250-employee cap. The reporting from White & Case and Lewis Silkin calls this "small mid-cap" coverage.

**ALEX:** Which captures a huge range of companies that the original law had effectively treated the same as large enterprises. A 400-person fintech is not the same as Goldman Sachs, and the compliance burden shouldn't be identical.

**JORDAN:** Now, here's where the Act got stricter, not looser. A new prohibited practice was added: AI systems used to generate non-consensual sexual or intimate content — including against minors — are now explicitly banned, with enforcement starting December 2026. That's one of the faster timelines in the entire regulation.

**ALEX:** And transparency requirements for AI-generated content actually tightened. The implementation grace period was cut from six months to three. New deadline: also December 2026.

**JORDAN:** So the pattern here is: more time for complex enterprise compliance, faster action on direct consumer harms. That's actually a coherent regulatory tradeoff, and it's more nuanced than the "EU backs down" framing you'll see in some of the coverage.

**ALEX:** The co-legislators intend to formally adopt the package before August 2nd — the date when existing high-risk system rules were scheduled to kick in. So the whole point is to pass the amendment before the original deadline lands.

**JORDAN:** If you're a practitioner building AI systems that touch any of those high-risk categories, this buys you time. Use it to get your documentation in order, because the requirements themselves haven't changed — just the clock.

**ALEX:** Up next: the agentic coding revolution is here, it's growing fast, and 88% of enterprise deployments are still failing before they reach production.

## SEGMENT 4 — AGENTIC CODING: REAL, FAST, AND MOSTLY FAILING IN ENTERPRISES

**JORDAN:** Let's talk about what's actually happening in software development right now, because the pace of change here is genuinely unusual.

Every major AI coding tool has completed what the industry is calling the "agentic pivot." GitHub introduced Agent Mode with multi-agent workflows in February. Cursor shipped background agents running on isolated VMs — your code is being edited by a process you didn't trigger. Replit's Agent 3 extended autonomous runtime to 200 minutes. Anthropic's Claude Code, which we mentioned in the Anthropic segment, is now a major revenue driver.

**ALEX:** The market numbers support this. The AI coding assistant market hit roughly $8.5 billion in 2026. 84% of developers now use or plan to use AI coding tools, and more than half use them daily. These are not niche adoption figures.

**JORDAN:** And the model economics have moved dramatically. GPT-5.5 came in at $2.25 per million input tokens. Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite is $0.25. Enterprise-grade capability that would have cost thousands per month in 2024 now fits in a startup's AWS bill.

**ALEX:** So far, so bullish. Here's where the story gets complicated. Analysts at Gartner, IDC, and others have been looking at enterprise AI agent deployments — not just coding assistants, but the broader wave of autonomous AI agents being deployed inside organizations. The data is stark: 88% of autonomous agent pilots fail before they reach production rollout.

**JORDAN:** Let that sit for a moment. Nearly nine in ten.

**ALEX:** And the reasons are consistent across the failed deployments. Integration complexity: agents need clean, accessible data in target systems, and most enterprise data environments are not clean. Governance gaps: the organizations that deployed fastest also tended to skip building the control infrastructure before they scaled. And scope creep: teams trying to automate everything at once rather than finding one high-volume workflow and proving it there.

**JORDAN:** Gartner goes further and says over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled entirely by the end of 2027. That's a prediction about projects that are alive today.

**ALEX:** Which does not mean the technology is failing. It means the deployment playbook is failing. There's a meaningful difference.

**JORDAN:** The analysts who are seeing successful enterprise deployments describe a consistent pattern: start with a single workflow that's high-volume and well-defined, make sure your data is accessible and governed before you deploy, and build human oversight controls before you try to remove them. The organizations hitting value fastest are the ones that treated agentic AI like any other complex infrastructure deployment, not like a product they could just install and run.

**ALEX:** The coding-specific tooling is actually performing better than general enterprise agents, probably because code is more structured and the feedback loop — does this code work or not — is cleaner than in other business processes.

**JORDAN:** For practitioners: the tooling is genuinely good now. Claude Code, Cursor, GitHub's agent mode — they are meaningfully better than what existed eighteen months ago. The risk isn't the tools. The risk is deploying without the governance infrastructure to manage what they produce.

**ALEX:** Watch for the Claude Code revenue breakdown when Anthropic's IPO filing comes out later this year. That number is going to tell us a lot about where the real value is accruing in the agentic stack.
