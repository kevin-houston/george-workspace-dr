## SEGMENT 1 — The Pentagon's Eight and the One It Left Out

**Jordan:** So the Pentagon story broke last Thursday, and the headline is that the Defense Department has signed agreements with eight major technology companies to deploy AI across its most sensitive classified military networks. We're talking SpaceX, OpenAI, Google, Nvidia, Microsoft, Amazon Web Services, Oracle, and a company called Reflection.

**Alex:** And the story is partly about who's in — but mostly about who isn't. Anthropic was explicitly excluded. The Pentagon designated Anthropic a "supply chain risk" — a label that has historically been reserved for companies with ties to foreign adversaries like China or Russia.

**Jordan:** Which is a remarkable thing to say about a U.S. AI safety company. The reason Anthropic was excluded is that they refused to agree to the Pentagon's blanket terms requiring that AI tools be available for, quote, "any lawful purpose." Anthropic wanted specific guarantees that Claude wouldn't be used for fully autonomous weapons systems or domestic mass surveillance.

**Alex:** And the Pentagon said no.

**Jordan:** The Pentagon said no. And then labeled them a supply chain risk for it. A federal judge in California has since blocked that designation, so Anthropic isn't formally blacklisted at the moment — but the underlying dispute hasn't been resolved.

**Alex:** Here's where it gets genuinely strange. Even while this blacklisting drama is playing out, the NSA is reportedly already using Anthropic's Claude Mythos — that's Anthropic's new cybersecurity-focused model — on classified networks. So the government is simultaneously blocking and using the same company's products.

**Jordan:** Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei visited the White House last month after Mythos launched. CNBC confirmed Friday that the Pentagon tech chief says Anthropic remains blacklisted — but also acknowledged the NSA situation.

**Alex:** For developers and builders in the audience — this matters beyond the drama. It's a signal that the national security community is setting norms for how AI systems will be deployed in the highest-stakes environments. The companies that agreed to the Pentagon's terms are now inside a very significant closed loop.

**Jordan:** And the companies that draw ethical lines — even ones that seem pretty reasonable, like "don't build fully autonomous weapons" — risk being locked out of the largest single buyer of technology on the planet.

**Alex:** Google employees, by the way, reportedly objected internally to that company's participation in the deal. Multiple sources confirmed there was significant dissent inside Google about signing on.

**Jordan:** Which tells you something about where this is heading. The gap between what AI companies' engineers want to build and what their government customers want to deploy is not closing.

## SEGMENT 2 — Stanford's AI Index: The Numbers That Don't Add Up Neatly

**Alex:** Every year Stanford's Institute for Human-Centered AI drops what is essentially the annual physical for the AI industry. This year's report came out last week and the headline number — the one everyone is talking about — is that autonomous AI agents completed 77.3% of real-world tasks successfully. That's up from 20% just twelve months ago.

**Jordan:** That's a fourfold improvement in one year. That's not incremental. If you're building anything that involves agents doing work on your behalf — browsing the web, writing code, managing files — that number should get your attention.

**Alex:** At the same time, the report documents what I think is one of the more alarming structural trends in American AI: the flow of AI researchers immigrating to the U.S. has dropped 89% since 2017. And 80% of that decline happened in just the last year.

**Jordan:** Eighty percent in one year. Stanford frames this as a "structural vulnerability that investment alone cannot offset." You can build a hundred new data centers, but you can't fast-track the people who actually know how to push the field forward.

**Alex:** And there's a jobs dimension to this that deserves attention. Software developer employment for workers aged 22 to 25 — so early-career engineers — has dropped nearly 20% since 2024. Mid-career and senior developers have held steady or grown. AI is not eliminating software engineering broadly; it's compressing the entry point.

**Jordan:** Which has real consequences for the next generation of experienced engineers. The path from junior to senior has always run through those early years of learning by doing. If that pipeline is disrupted, we don't feel the downstream effects immediately — we feel them in about five years.

**Alex:** Two more numbers from the report that I think belong together. First: global corporate AI investment hit $581.7 billion in 2025 — up 130% year-over-year. Second: the Foundation Model Transparency Index dropped from 58 to 40 points. The most capable models are disclosing the least information about how they were built.

**Jordan:** So more money, less transparency. The companies getting the biggest checks are sharing the least about what they're actually building.

**Alex:** And then there's the gap between expert and public perception. 59% of people say they feel optimistic about AI's potential benefits. 52% say they feel nervous. Those numbers can both be true at the same time, and the Stanford data suggests most people are holding both feelings simultaneously.

**Jordan:** Which is actually the correct response. These systems are genuinely impressive and genuinely hard to understand. Holding both at once seems like the honest position.

## SEGMENT 3 — The $7 Trillion Bet and the Bill That Comes With It

**Jordan:** Let's talk about the numbers that make everything else in this episode possible. The five largest U.S. cloud and AI infrastructure companies — that's Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, and Oracle — are on track to spend between $660 and $690 billion on capital expenditure in 2026. That's nearly double what they spent in 2025.

**Alex:** The World Economic Forum, in an April analysis, put the total projected investment in AI data center infrastructure through 2030 at $7 trillion, with McKinsey estimating $5.2 trillion of that dedicated specifically to AI workloads.

**Jordan:** For context: tech capital expenditure is now running at 1.9% of U.S. GDP. The WEF report notes that's comparable in scale to the combined investment of the Interstate Highway System and the Apollo Program.

**Alex:** That's not a typo. The infrastructure build-out happening right now is on that historical scale. MIT Technology Review named hyperscale AI data centers one of their ten breakthrough technologies of 2026.

**Jordan:** And this brings us to the part of the story that doesn't get enough attention: the energy bill. Stanford's AI Index reports that AI data centers now demand 29.6 gigawatts of power. That's comparable to New York State's peak electricity consumption.

**Alex:** And it's growing. Grok 4's training run — one model training — emitted an estimated 72,816 tons of CO2. That's roughly equivalent to putting 17,000 cars on the road for a year. From one training run.

**Jordan:** There's a real question being asked in infrastructure circles right now: is the AI industry building faster than the grid can support it? The answer in many U.S. markets is yes. We're seeing data center construction outpacing power utility build-out in Virginia, Texas, and Arizona.

**Alex:** The geopolitical dimension is also significant. The EU has an announced €200 billion AI Continent Action Plan. Saudi Arabia has committed over $40 billion. China announced a $47.5 billion state-led semiconductor fund. This isn't just a corporate race — it's a national infrastructure competition.

**Jordan:** And the U.S. is ahead — for now. The Stanford Index notes that the U.S. invests 23 times more in AI than China. But China's best models are now trading places at the top of global performance rankings with U.S. models. Anthropic's current lead is 2.7 percentage points on the key benchmarks — which is not a comfortable margin.

**Alex:** The gap between who's spending the most and who's winning is narrowing. That's the story of this infrastructure moment.

## SEGMENT 4 — The AI Governance Standoff

**Jordan:** The last story today is the regulatory picture, and it is genuinely complicated. In March, the White House released what it's calling a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence. It's a set of legislative recommendations to Congress — nonbinding for now — but it lays out what the administration wants federal AI governance to look like.

**Alex:** The centerpiece is federal preemption. The administration is recommending that Congress pass legislation that would prevent states from imposing regulations on AI that it considers, quote, "undue burdens." The goal is a single national standard rather than fifty different state-level regimes.

**Jordan:** And the stated rationale makes sense on its face: AI systems don't respect state borders. A model trained in California and deployed by a company in Texas to users in Florida shouldn't have to navigate fifty different compliance regimes.

**Alex:** But here's the tension. States are where most of the actual binding regulation is happening right now. Colorado passed the most comprehensive state-level AI governance law in the country, and it goes into effect June 30th — less than two months from now. It requires developers and deployers of high-risk AI systems to run risk management programs, make consumer disclosures, and mitigate algorithmic discrimination.

**Jordan:** And the White House framework, if enacted, could preempt exactly that.

**Alex:** Democrats in Congress have responded with the GUARDRAILS Act — which would explicitly block federal preemption of state AI safety laws. So you have a genuine legislative standoff where the administration wants to clear the field for AI development and members of Congress want to preserve the right of states to set safety floors.

**Jordan:** The framework also recommends against creating any new federal regulatory body for AI. Instead of a dedicated agency, the administration says existing regulators — the FTC, FDA, SEC — should handle AI within their existing jurisdictions.

**Alex:** Which is fine if the existing regulatory machinery is adequate to the task. The open question is whether a regulator built to oversee 20th century financial products or pharmaceutical approvals has the tools to keep pace with systems that can autonomously write code, make medical recommendations, and operate military infrastructure.

**Jordan:** The EU has a dedicated AI Act with its own enforcement structure. The U.S. is explicitly choosing not to build the equivalent. Whether that turns out to be smart regulatory flexibility or a consequential gap in oversight — we won't know for a few years.

**Alex:** And by then, a lot of deployment decisions will already be irreversible.

**Jordan:** That's the thing about fault lines. You only know where they were after the ground moves.
