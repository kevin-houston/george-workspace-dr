## SEGMENT 3: THE FEDERAL AI BILL — CONGRESS DRAWS LINES, STATES PUSH BACK

**JORDAN:** Yesterday — Thursday, June 4th — two House lawmakers dropped a 269-page draft bill that would create the first comprehensive federal framework for artificial intelligence in the United States. It's bipartisan: Republican Jay Obernolte from California and Democrat Lori Trahan from Massachusetts.

**ALEX:** Here's what the bill does. It requires top AI developers — think frontier models, the Claudes and GPT-5s of the world — to create and implement plans addressing potentially catastrophic risks from their systems, including supercharged cybersecurity threats. Third-party auditors would verify that companies actually follow through.

**JORDAN:** And it requires disclosure of safety and security risks before new powerful models ship. Trump separately signed an executive order on Tuesday asking companies to voluntarily submit new models for government cybersecurity review thirty days before release. The bill would put teeth on a version of that idea.

**ALEX:** But the piece of this that has ignited a political firestorm is state preemption. The Obernolte-Trahan bill would override state-level AI laws — meaning California's rules, New York's rules, Illinois's rules — all get pushed aside in favor of a single federal standard.

**JORDAN:** And the backlash has been fierce, and it's coming from both parties. State lawmakers in Massachusetts have publicly warned their own congresswoman against backing this bill. New York legislators are warning the same. Ron DeSantis called the earlier version of federal preemption, which included a decade-long moratorium on state AI rules, quote, "AI amnesty."

**ALEX:** Brad Carson from Americans for Responsible Innovation put it more pointedly. He said the current bill would turn "the current floor on state AI legislation into a ceiling." The concern: if federal law says this is the maximum, states can never do more — and the federal baseline might be too weak.

**JORDAN:** On the other side, the industry generally prefers a single federal standard over fifty different state regulations. And there's a real argument that conflicting state rules could create a compliance nightmare that actually chills AI development and deployment.

**ALEX:** Here's the political context that makes this urgent: Politico is calling this the last realistic chance to pass federal AI rules before the midterm elections. After the midterms, the legislative environment changes. Whatever gets written this summer is probably what we're living with for the next several years.

**JORDAN:** And for developers and enterprises building AI products right now, the preemption question is the most practically important. If you're building a hiring algorithm, a credit-scoring model, an AI medical tool — the legal landscape you're operating in depends entirely on whether states can supplement federal standards or not.

**ALEX:** The bill still has to get through committee, then the full House, then the Senate. But the fact that it's bipartisan and moving now — that's more progress than Washington has made on AI legislation in the previous three years combined.

**JORDAN:** Worth watching closely. We'll link the full draft text in the show notes.

## SEGMENT 4: AGENTIC AI — FROM HYPE CYCLE TO ENTERPRISE REALITY

**ALEX:** Let's close with something that's been building for the last year and has now, according to Gartner, hit an inflection point. We're talking about agentic AI — AI systems that don't just answer questions but take sequences of actions, make decisions autonomously, and run for hours without human intervention.

**JORDAN:** Gartner published its 2026 Hype Cycle for Agentic AI back in April, and it's telling a different story than last year's. The headline finding: governance, security, and cost-focused profiles are now distributed across the hype curve alongside core agentic AI technologies.

**ALEX:** Translate that for me.

**JORDAN:** Last year, agentic AI was a single phenomenon — "AI that does things." Now the market is sophisticated enough to break it into component problems: how do you govern these systems? How do you secure them when they're making API calls and writing to databases without human review? How do you manage the cost when a single agent task can consume a hundred or a thousand times more compute than a chat response?

**ALEX:** Those governance and security questions moving to the front of the hype cycle means enterprise buyers are no longer evaluating agentic AI in the abstract — they're evaluating specific implementations and they're finding them wanting in specific ways.

**JORDAN:** And yet adoption is accelerating. The $9B enterprise market analysis published in March put the compound growth rate at over 40 percent annually. McKinsey's latest numbers show adoption rates tripling in twelve months in financial services and software development, which are the two sectors moving fastest.

**ALEX:** The pattern that's emerging in the field — and this is something practitioners are talking about more than analysts — is what you might call the long-context agent. These are systems that run for hours or even days on a single task, spinning up specialized sub-agents, managing their own memory, and only surfacing to a human when they hit a genuine decision fork.

**JORDAN:** We've gone from "AI that can browse the web" to "AI that can run a multi-week software development sprint and only ask you questions when something is genuinely ambiguous." That's a qualitatively different thing.

**ALEX:** The catch — and Gartner is pretty explicit about this — is that the infrastructure for managing these agents at enterprise scale doesn't fully exist yet. Observability tools, audit logging, cost governors, safety rails that work across multi-step reasoning chains — those are still being built. The agent capability has outrun the management tooling.

**JORDAN:** Which is actually a familiar pattern in tech. The compute shows up first, the governance catches up later. We saw it with cloud, with mobile, with APIs. The question is whether AI moves fast enough that governance arrives before the first major incident that causes a regulatory overreaction.

**ALEX:** Given our segment on the Congressional AI bill — that's not a hypothetical concern.

**JORDAN:** Exactly. For developers in the audience: the practical upshot is that if you're building with agentic AI right now, the moat is not in the model choice — it's in the orchestration layer. How do you build workflows that are transparent enough to audit, cost-controlled enough to scale, and safe enough that when the agents do something unexpected, you have a recovery path?

**ALEX:** The teams solving those problems today are the ones enterprises are going to be calling in six months.
