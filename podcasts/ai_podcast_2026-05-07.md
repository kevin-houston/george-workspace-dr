## SEGMENT 1 — Microsoft Agent 365: Agents as the Operating System

**Alex:** So Microsoft made something official on May 1st: Microsoft Agent 365 is now generally available. And the way they're framing it is striking — they're calling agents the new operating layer for work.

**Jordan:** And Satya Nadella has been building toward this metaphor for a while. The idea is that in the same way an operating system manages hardware resources — memory, CPU, storage — the agent layer manages cognitive resources: knowledge, workflows, decisions, across the Microsoft cloud.

**Alex:** So Copilot, which started as a sidebar helper, has become an orchestrator of autonomous agents. Digital workers that can reason over business processes, execute multi-step tasks, and collaborate with each other.

**Jordan:** The governance piece is new and interesting. Agent 365 includes a dedicated admin console for IT departments to deploy, manage, and secure agents at scale. Which is a real acknowledgment that the chaotic, shadow-IT era of everyone using random AI tools is over. Microsoft wants to be the control plane.

**Alex:** And the adoption numbers in their 2026 Work Trend Index report are extraordinary. They're reporting that 78% of knowledge workers now use AI agents at least weekly. That's up from 12% in 2024.

**Jordan:** That's a seventy-eight percent to twelve percent move in two years. Even accounting for how they define "knowledge workers" and what counts as "using an agent" — that's a category-level shift.

**Alex:** The pricing is also notable. Agent 365 is positioned at fifteen dollars per user per month, separate from the existing Microsoft 365 Copilot licensing. And they're not powering it all with one model — four different models underpin different parts of the service.

**Jordan:** Which is the multi-model world in practice. You don't run everything on the most expensive model. You route tasks to the right model at the right cost. That's the architecture that makes agents economically viable at scale.

**Alex:** The pushback I keep seeing is: okay, but are these agents actually doing meaningful work, or are they filling in forms slightly faster? Gartner is projecting that 40% of enterprise AI agent projects are at risk of failure by 2027 because of "messy governance and unclear ROI."

**Jordan:** And Microsoft's answer to that is: that's why you need Agent 365. The governance console. The audit trail. The ability to actually see what your agents are doing. Whether that solves the ROI problem is a separate question, but the product is at least pointed at the right problem.

**Alex:** Satya Nadella has said this is the biggest platform shift Microsoft has seen since cloud. If he's right, agents as the operating layer is a very large bet. And as of May 1st, it's not a bet anymore — it's a shipping product.

## SEGMENT 2 — The TAKE IT DOWN Act: A Compliance Deadline Is Coming

**Jordan:** Let's talk about a law that most people haven't heard of but that is about to matter a great deal to anyone running an online platform. The TAKE IT DOWN Act.

**Alex:** The full name is the Tools to Address Known Exploitation by Immobilizing Technological Deepfakes on Websites and Networks Act, which is why everyone just calls it the TAKE IT DOWN Act.

**Jordan:** It was signed into law in May 2025 — so about a year ago — and the criminal prohibition on publishing nonconsensual intimate images and deepfakes took effect immediately. But platforms had one year to build their compliance infrastructure.

**Alex:** That one year is up on May 19th, 2026. Twelve days from today.

**Jordan:** So any "covered platform" — websites, apps, online services — now has to have a working notice-and-removal process in place. When someone notifies you that a nonconsensual intimate image or deepfake of them is on your platform, you have 48 hours to take it down.

**Alex:** Forty-eight hours is extremely aggressive. Most DMCA takedown processes operate on a timeline of days to weeks. This is two business days.

**Jordan:** And the scope is broader than just sexual deepfakes. The law also covers any deepfake "intended to cause harm" — which is deliberately broad and will take court interpretation to define at the edges.

**Alex:** What I find interesting is what this does to the infrastructure problem. If you're running a platform with user-generated content at any meaningful scale, you now have a legal obligation to surveil your own platform for this category of content and respond in 48 hours.

**Jordan:** Which means you're probably buying AI detection tools to do that surveillance. You're staffing a moderation team that can respond 24/7. You're building an intake pipeline for notifications. This isn't a policy checkbox — it's an operational system.

**Alex:** Schools are an interesting case here. Fisher Phillips put out guidance specifically for educational institutions saying: four steps you must take. Because schools now operate online platforms — learning management systems, communication tools — and they're also dealing with this problem among students. It hits them on multiple dimensions.

**Jordan:** The law has been framed primarily around the victims, and that's right — the harm these images cause is real and documented. But the practical reality is that this is one of the first AI-specific legal obligations with teeth that applies broadly to platform operators, not just AI developers.

**Alex:** It's also the first federal law in the U.S. that directly criminalizes a specific AI output. Deepfakes of this type are now federally prohibited, full stop. That's new legal territory.

**Jordan:** And it comes as the broader AI regulatory landscape in the U.S. remains fragmented — state laws racing ahead, federal framework still developing. The TAKE IT DOWN Act is a rare case where federal law moved first and moved fast. The compliance clock is now.

## SEGMENT 3 — $700 Billion and a Grid That Wasn't Built for This

**Alex:** Let's talk about infrastructure. Because the scale of what is being built right now is genuinely difficult to wrap your head around.

**Jordan:** According to Fortune's reporting from April 30th, the five largest U.S. cloud and AI infrastructure companies have committed between 660 and 700 billion dollars in capital expenditure for 2026 alone. That is nearly double their 2025 level.

**Alex:** For reference: the entire Interstate Highway System cost about 530 billion dollars in today's dollars over the decades it was built. AI infrastructure this year is exceeding that.

**Jordan:** And the IEA — the International Energy Agency — reported this week that data center electricity use grew 17% globally in 2025. AI-specific data centers grew even faster. Global electricity demand as a whole grew about three percent.

**Alex:** So you have a sector whose electricity demand is growing at five times the rate of the overall grid. And the grid is old. Much of U.S. transmission infrastructure was built in the 1970s and 1980s.

**Jordan:** The interconnection queue number is the one that really stopped me. The queue of projects waiting to connect to the U.S. grid has ballooned to over 2,100 gigawatts. That exceeds total current U.S. generating capacity. You have more projects waiting to connect than the grid can actually supply.

**Alex:** Which means timelines slip. And they're slipping dramatically — industry analysis projects that 30 to 50 percent of planned 2026 data center capacity will not actually come online until 2028 at the earliest.

**Jordan:** That's not a rounding error. That's half the planned build potentially delayed by two years because the physical grid can't keep up with the capital commitments.

**Alex:** There's also a consumer angle that's easy to miss. A PowerLines analysis published in April looked at 51 utilities serving 250 million customers and found that U.S. utilities are now projecting $1.4 trillion in total infrastructure spending. Roughly half of that — around $700 billion — gets passed to residential customers through rate hikes.

**Jordan:** So AI infrastructure spending isn't just an abstract number about tech companies. It's the reason your electric bill might be higher in 2028 than it is today.

**Alex:** The counterargument — and I think it's worth taking seriously — is that the U.S. grid needed modernization anyway. The AI build is forcing that investment faster than it would have happened on its own. The question is whether the pace is manageable or whether it creates reliability risks in the interim.

**Jordan:** And whether the ratepayer distribution is fair. The companies generating the demand and the companies paying the bills aren't the same people. That's the political tension that's going to run through energy policy for the next several years.

**Alex:** The numbers are large enough that this has moved from a tech story to a utilities story to an infrastructure policy story. It's all three simultaneously.

## SEGMENT 4 — OpenAI at $25 Billion and a Very Expensive IPO Question

**Jordan:** Let's close with the OpenAI story, which has a couple of layers to it.

**Alex:** So OpenAI crossed $25 billion in annualized revenue earlier this year — February, by most accounts. That number was confirmed by CFO Sarah Friar in filings and public comments. They also recently closed a funding round at an $852 billion valuation.

**Jordan:** For scale: OpenAI hit $2 billion in annualized revenue in 2023, $6 billion in 2024, $20 billion at the end of 2025, $25 billion now. That's a revenue trajectory that has very few parallels in business history.

**Alex:** Salesforce took 18 years to reach $25 billion in annual revenue. Google took 17. Facebook took 12. OpenAI did it in roughly three years.

**Jordan:** And yet — it's not profitable. The compute costs, the talent costs, the infrastructure commitments eat the revenue. So the question of going public is complicated.

**Alex:** The internal debate, according to reporting from TechTimes earlier this week, is a genuine disagreement between Sam Altman and Sarah Friar. Altman wants to go public in 2026. Friar — and several board members — are advocating for 2027, citing missed growth targets and what the reporting described as $600 billion in infrastructure commitments that create significant financial uncertainty.

**Jordan:** And that $600 billion figure makes sense in the context of the infrastructure story we just told. OpenAI is one of the companies making massive commitments to compute capacity that doesn't exist yet. If data center timelines slip — which we just said they likely will — that creates real exposure.

**Alex:** The IPO math is also complicated by the structure of OpenAI's capped-profit model, which is in the middle of a conversion to a more conventional corporate structure. That conversion isn't complete, and going public before it is creates legal and regulatory complexity.

**Jordan:** So you have the world's fastest-growing software company, sitting at $852 billion valuation, with 900 million users and $25 billion in revenue, and the CEO and CFO publicly can't agree on whether to go public this year or next.

**Alex:** Which tells you something about how genuinely uncertain the financial picture is even at this level. Revenue is not the constraint. Profitability is. Infrastructure commitments are. The timeline question is real.

**Jordan:** For developers and builders, the short-term practical answer is: this doesn't change much. The APIs are running, the models are getting better, the pricing is competitive. The corporate finance question of when Altman rings the NYSE bell is somewhat removed from product decisions.

**Alex:** But the longer-term implication is interesting. If OpenAI goes public, it will face quarterly earnings pressure, activist investors, and the short-term incentives that come with public market scrutiny. That might change how they make product decisions in ways that matter to the developer ecosystem.

**Jordan:** The IPO question is also a window into how strange this moment is. The most important AI company in the world, arguably the fastest-growing revenue story in tech history, and the inside conversation is: are we ready? That's a remarkable place to be.
