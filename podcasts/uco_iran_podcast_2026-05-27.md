# Why Is Crude Oil Down When Iran Is at War?
**A Deep Dive into UCO, the Hormuz Crisis, and the Forces Pulling Oil Lower**

*Runtime: ~10 minutes | Recorded: May 27, 2026*
*Hosts: Alex (male) and Jordan (female)*

---

## INTRO (~1 minute)

**Alex:** Welcome to Markets Unpacked. I'm Alex.

**Jordan:** And I'm Jordan. Today we're tackling a question that's been sitting at the top of a lot of traders' inboxes this week: why is crude oil actually falling — with an active shooting war centered on the world's most critical oil chokepoint?

**Alex:** We're talking about the Strait of Hormuz, the Iran conflict, and specifically UCO — the ProShares 2x leveraged crude oil ETF — which has shed more than four percent in a single session this week, closing around $45 on Tuesday, May 26th.

**Jordan:** If you bought UCO expecting war to be a rocket ship for oil prices, this episode is for you. We're going to break down the geopolitical picture, the supply and demand math, and then the UCO-specific mechanics that make this ETF a beast of its own.

## SEGMENT 1: UCO Price Action and the Paradox (~2.5 minutes)

**Alex:** So let's set the scene. On February 28th of this year, the United States and Israel launched strikes on Iran — kicking off what is now being called the 2026 Iran War. The Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly twenty percent of global oil and LNG normally flows, was effectively shut down as Iran threatened to attack any tankers attempting to cross.

**Jordan:** Oil prices absolutely spiked in the immediate aftermath. WTI surged well above a hundred dollars a barrel. UCO, which doubles the daily move of WTI crude futures, went along for the ride. People who bought in early made real money.

**Alex:** But here's the paradox. Three months in, with the war still active and the Strait still largely closed, crude is not at two hundred dollars a barrel. WTI fell more than four percent in a single day this week — according to Trading Economics, it dropped to around $88 per barrel on Wednesday May 27th — and UCO has been in a painful slide from its April highs.

**Jordan:** The 52-week range on UCO tells the story vividly. The ETF hit a low of around $18 earlier in its cycle and a high of roughly $44. Right now it's sitting near that upper end — but the directional pressure is clearly lower, not higher.

**Alex:** So what is the market pricing in that oil bulls are missing?

**Jordan:** Three things, and they're all happening simultaneously. First, peace deal speculation. Second, a demand shock from the war itself. And third — something specific to UCO that has nothing to do with oil prices at all.

**Alex:** Let's do the macro first and come back to the ETF mechanics. Jordan, walk us through what happened over the weekend that triggered this latest leg down.

**Jordan:** On late Saturday, May 24th, President Trump posted on Truth Social that a peace agreement between the US and Iran is "largely negotiated." He said the Strait of Hormuz would be reopened as part of the deal, and Iran would clear the mines it deployed in the strait. According to Gaik June Goh, a senior oil market analyst at Sparta Commodities, that was enough to pull front-month Brent futures down five percent at the Sunday futures open — to just below ninety-nine dollars a barrel.

**Alex:** That's the market saying: if the Strait reopens, a hundred million barrels of stranded oil sitting in tankers queued up near the Gulf floods the market simultaneously.

**Jordan:** Exactly. And the White House later called an Iranian state media report about a specific draft deal a "complete fabrication" — so there's a ton of uncertainty still. Multiple analysts Rigzone spoke to warned the deal looks fragile and could collapse. But markets don't need certainty. They need a credible probability. And right now, they're pricing in maybe a 40-to-50 percent chance of reopening within weeks.

**Alex:** The ceasefire-driven price drop is real even when the ceasefire isn't signed. That's the paradox in one sentence.

---

## SEGMENT 2: Why Oil Isn't Spiking — Supply and Demand Dynamics (~2.5 minutes)

**Jordan:** Now let's step back and look at the longer arc, because the peace deal speculation is only part of the story. There are structural supply and demand forces at work that are independently weighing on oil.

**Alex:** On the supply side, OPEC+ has been quietly adding barrels all year. In April they came back with a 206,000 barrel-per-day output increase — that's from Reuters in March. Then at their May 3rd meeting, they agreed to a third consecutive monthly hike of 188,000 barrels per day for June.

**Jordan:** And that's happening despite the Hormuz closure. The logic from Saudi Arabia and Russia seems to be: members who can export from non-Gulf routes — through pipelines, Red Sea alternatives, West African ports — should. It's a market-share play, and it keeps putting incremental supply into the global picture.

**Alex:** Al Jazeera called the May OPEC move a "symbolic gesture" — and Brent barely blinked. But death by a thousand cuts. Every extra barrel chips away at the supply crisis premium that the Hormuz closure created.

**Jordan:** The demand side is actually the more alarming part. The IEA's May 2026 Oil Market Report — which multiple outlets including Business Standard and Argus Media reported on — forecast global oil demand to contract by 420,000 barrels per day year-over-year in 2026. Not grow. Contract. To 104 million barrels per day.

**Alex:** And that contraction is 1.3 million barrels per day worse than the IEA's pre-war forecast. High energy prices are killing their own market.

**Jordan:** Right. VT Markets put it plainly: the US non-farm payrolls report for April showed an unexpected decline of 50,000 jobs, according to their analysis — hinting that high energy prices are beginning to slow the economy. Fear of recession is temporarily outweighing the physical reality of a supply shortage.

**Alex:** There's a phrase economists use: demand destruction. When oil gets expensive enough, people drive less, factories cut back, airlines retire routes. The war created a supply shock. The supply shock is now creating a demand shock. And the demand shock is looping back to suppress the price.

**Jordan:** The IEA still sees a supply deficit for 2026 overall. The physical market is tight. But the financial market — the futures market that UCO tracks — is forward-looking. And what it's looking forward to is slower growth, possible recession, and maybe a reopening of the Strait.

**Alex:** So you have a real supply crunch on one side, and recession fears plus peace hopes on the other. The bears are winning that tug of war right now.

**Jordan:** Add to that the Trump factor. Per CNBC, oil dropped about one percent just from Trump saying the war will end "very quickly." That was May 20th. The president's rhetoric alone moves oil markets, and he keeps saying the deal is close.

---

## SEGMENT 3: UCO-Specific Mechanics — Leverage Decay and Contango (~2 minutes)

**Alex:** Okay, now let's talk about the ETF itself. Because even if you're right about crude oil's direction long-term, UCO can still eat your lunch. This is the part a lot of retail investors skip.

**Jordan:** UCO is a 2x daily leveraged ETF. That means it targets twice the daily return of a crude oil futures index — specifically the Bloomberg Commodity Balanced WTI Crude Oil Index. Not twice the return of oil over a week, or a month, or a year. Daily.

**Alex:** That distinction is critical. Here's why. Imagine oil falls 10 percent one day. UCO falls 20 percent. Now oil rallies 10 percent the next day. UCO gains 20 percent. You're back where you started on oil — but UCO is at 96 cents on the dollar, not a dollar. The math just doesn't recover symmetrically.

**Jordan:** That's called volatility decay or beta slippage. It's baked into every leveraged ETF. The more volatile the underlying — and oil is famously volatile — the more the decay costs you over time.

**Alex:** Then there's contango. This is a futures-market structure issue. If the futures curve is in contango — meaning next month's contract is more expensive than this month's — the fund has to sell the cheap near-term contract and buy the expensive far-dated one every month.

**Jordan:** You're perpetually selling low and buying high just to stay invested. That roll cost can be anywhere from a fraction of a percent to several percent per month, depending on how steep the contango is. For a 2x fund, every drag on the underlying gets doubled.

**Alex:** Right now, analysts at 247 Wall St. and others have noted that when oil is in uncertainty — prices spiking and crashing on each rumor — the curve can flip between backwardation and contango rapidly. That instability itself is a cost to UCO holders.

**Jordan:** The bottom line: UCO is a legitimate short-term trading instrument. It's well-suited for a trader who thinks oil is going up tomorrow and wants double exposure. It is not a buy-and-hold vehicle. It is not a way to express a long-term view on the Iran conflict. Over six to twelve months, structural decay can mean UCO lags raw crude significantly — sometimes dramatically — even if oil goes up.

**Alex:** The analytics site Bitget put it plainly: "Even if spot oil is range-bound, persistent contango can make UCO drift lower over weeks and months." You don't need the commodity to fall. The fund can fall on its own.

---

## OUTRO + TAKEAWAYS (~1.5 minutes)

**Jordan:** Let's bring it home. The paradox of crude oil falling while Iran is at war resolves once you hold all the pieces together at once.

**Alex:** First: war premium is already priced in. Markets priced in the Hormuz disruption back in February and March when prices spiked. What moves markets now is the delta — the change from the current expectation. And the current delta is peace negotiations that are progressing, however fitfully.

**Jordan:** Second: demand is being destroyed by the very prices the war created. The IEA is forecasting a year-over-year contraction in global oil demand for 2026 — that's an extraordinary statement. High prices are working. Demand is falling.

**Alex:** Third: OPEC+ keeps adding barrels. Three consecutive monthly output hikes signal that the cartel believes the market can absorb more supply, and that they want market share back.

**Jordan:** And fourth — specific to UCO — the ETF has structural headwinds that exist entirely apart from oil's price. Volatility decay and contango roll costs compound against holders the longer they stay in. This is a tool, not a position.

**Alex:** The physical oil market still has a real supply deficit. Sparta Commodities estimates it could take three to six months to normalize flows even after the Strait reopens. If peace talks collapse — as multiple analysts warned this week they might — oil could spike hard again.

**Jordan:** So the situation remains genuinely volatile and hard to call. What's clear is that "war = higher oil prices" is a much simpler story than what's actually happening.

**Alex:** Thanks for listening to Markets Unpacked. If this episode was useful, share it with someone who's holding UCO and wondering why it's not cooperating.

**Jordan:** We'll be back next week. Stay curious out there.
