## SEGMENT 1 — Google I/O 2026: Gemini Everywhere

These are lightweight glasses with cameras, microphones, and speakers, paired to an Android phone, with Gemini 2.5 Pro running in the background.

**Alex:** There's an optional in-lens display for private, contextual information — navigation overlays, live translation, that kind of thing. And Google lined up serious hardware partners: Samsung, Warby Parker, Gentle Monster, and XREAL.

**Jordan:** Warby Parker is the one that gets me. That's a signal Google is serious about mainstream pricing. Not a $3,000 developer kit.

**Jordan:** The other big announcement is what they're calling Gemini Intelligence — an agentic layer baked directly into Android 17. The demo they've been teasing shows you speaking a request to your phone and Gemini reaching across apps to get it done, without you having to navigate anything.

**Alex:** Which sounds like Siri promises from 2011, but people who've seen the actual preview say the cross-app reasoning is genuinely different. The new "Create My Widget" feature in Android 17 is a small example — you describe what you want, Gemini builds a custom widget for your home screen.

**Jordan:** And then there's Googlebooks — which is Google's answer to the MacBook. Premium Android-powered laptops replacing Chromebooks, with partners including Acer, ASUS, and Lenovo. The branding alone is a statement — they're going after the laptop market directly.

Google is landing all of this in a week when their own Gemini models have been ranked behind Anthropic's Claude Mythos and various GPT-5 variants on the major AI leaderboards. The hardware play is partly about making Gemini's ambient presence the advantage, even if the raw model scores aren't at the top.

**Jordan:** It's a classic Google move — win the distribution battle even when you're not winning the benchmark battle.

## SEGMENT 2 — The $700 Billion Infrastructure Question

**Jordan:** Okay, let's talk about the bill for all of this AI. Fortune published a story in late April with a number that genuinely stops you cold: big tech will spend over $700 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026 alone.

**Alex:** That's Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, and Meta — the four hyperscalers — and that figure is just their capital expenditure for this year. Amazon alone has publicly committed $200 billion.

**Jordan:** McKinsey projects $7 trillion in data center investment through 2030, with $5.2 trillion of that specifically for AI workloads. At the end of September 2025, over 23 gigawatts of data center capacity was under construction globally, with about three-quarters of it in the United States.

**Alex:** To put 23 gigawatts in perspective — the Hoover Dam generates about 2 gigawatts. We're talking about the equivalent of more than eleven Hoover Dams worth of new computing capacity, under construction simultaneously.

**Jordan:** And the concentration is striking. The World Economic Forum noted in April that as AI models scale, demand for compute increases exponentially — creating what they called a "systemic dependency on high-end semiconductors, massive data center complexes, and stable electricity supplies."

**Alex:** Which is why Nvidia has been making these unusual vertical investments. They've agreed to invest up to $3.2 billion in Corning — the glass manufacturer — and up to $2.1 billion in IREN, a data center operator. Nvidia is staking out positions up and down the supply chain for its own chips.

**Jordan:** And we should mention: a single NVIDIA H100 GPU costs roughly $40,000. The training runs for frontier models now require tens of thousands of those chips running for months.

**Alex:** There are real questions about where this ends. Goldman Sachs has been tracking what they call "the assumptions shaping the scale of the AI build-out" — and they note that the optimistic scenario requires AI to drive productivity gains large enough to justify not just the compute costs but the energy and water consumption of operating these facilities.

**Jordan:** The pessimistic scenario is that a lot of this capacity ends up underutilized — the way 5G rollout has been. But unlike 5G, AI workloads are already consuming the capacity that exists. It's hard to argue demand is speculative when the GPU queues at every cloud provider are months long.

**Alex:** The shift underway is from AI as software to AI as physical infrastructure. And the companies who get the infrastructure right — the chips, the power, the cooling, the fiber — may end up in a position analogous to railroads in the 1880s. The picks and shovels story.

## SEGMENT 3 — EU AI Act: 74 Days to Full Enforcement

**Jordan:** Let's talk regulation. If you're building anything with a large language model and you have any European users, August 2, 2026 is a date that should be on your calendar.

**Alex:** That's the date the EU AI Act's enforcement powers for General-Purpose AI models go fully into effect. The EU AI Office will have authority to demand information, order model recalls, mandate risk mitigations, and impose fines on GPAI providers — including non-EU companies.

**Jordan:** And the fines are not symbolic. Non-compliance with GPAI obligations carries penalties of up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher. For a company the size of OpenAI or Anthropic at their current valuations, 3% of global revenue is a significant number.

**Alex:** The prohibited practices — things like social scoring systems, certain biometric uses, subliminal manipulation — have actually been enforceable since February 2025, with even higher penalties: €35 million or 7% of global revenue.

**Jordan:** Finland became the first EU member state with fully operational national AI Act enforcement on January 1st of this year. Other member states are following.

**Alex:** What's new this month is the compliance pressure is hitting boardrooms hard. A piece in Foreign Policy Journal last week described it as "a compliance environment with no obvious precedent in modern corporate governance." The EU AI Act, the UK's sector-by-sector AI rules, and SEC disclosure requirements around AI are all converging simultaneously.

**Jordan:** The White House also released a National Policy Framework for AI in March 2026 — a set of legislative recommendations aimed at creating a nationally unified US approach. That's not law yet, but it signals the direction.

**Alex:** The practical challenge for developers is that the GPAI obligations require transparency documentation, copyright compliance policies, and systemic risk assessments. For startups building on top of foundation model APIs, the question is: how much of that burden flows down to you versus staying with the foundation model provider?

**Jordan:** The answer, at least under the current EU guidance, is that most of the GPAI obligations sit with the providers of the foundation models — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google — not the downstream application builders. But "downstream fine-tuners" who modify models in material ways may pick up obligations of their own.

**Alex:** If you're building in this space, August 2nd is real. The EU AI Office is not the FTC.

## SEGMENT 4 — Agentic AI: When Agents Can Act, Governance Becomes Urgent

**Jordan:** Our last story is less about a specific announcement and more about a tipping point. Gartner's latest forecast says 40% of enterprise applications will include task-specific AI agents by the end of 2026 — up from less than 5% in 2025.

**Alex:** That's an enormous shift in a short time. And Gartner also says 40% of those agentic AI projects are at risk of failure by 2027 — because of messy governance, unclear ROI, and insufficient security controls.

**Jordan:** The governance issues are real and somewhat underreported. There were several notable launches this month specifically targeting the problem. WSO2 launched Agent Manager on May 5th — a platform for enterprises to identify, govern, secure, and scale AI agents across environments.

**Alex:** Incredibuild launched Islo on May 4th — a sandboxing system for running AI coding agents in isolated environments. The insight there is that engineering teams want the automation benefits but don't want to give agents broad system access.

**Jordan:** And Notion launched a Developer Platform on May 13th with Workers and an External Agent API — letting teams deploy agents with access to live Notion data. Which sounds useful, but also means an external agent can now read and write your company's internal documents if you misconfigure permissions.

**Alex:** That's the core tension. The moment an AI agent can log in, move money, send email on your behalf, or access files — the risk profile changes entirely. It's no longer a chatbot making a mistake in a UI. It's a system taking real actions in the world.

**Jordan:** The Agentic AI Foundation — which now has 180 member organizations — has been pushing open agent standards precisely because the vendor-specific implementations create incompatible governance models. If one team is running Microsoft Copilot agents and another is running AWS Bedrock agents, your security and audit tooling breaks.

**Alex:** The biggest unresolved question is identity. How do you audit what an AI agent did, and on whose authority? When an agent acts on behalf of an employee, who is responsible for the outcome?

**Jordan:** The companies getting this right are the ones treating agent deployment like they treat human contractor onboarding — minimum necessary permissions, clear scope of action, audit logging, easy revocation.

**Alex:** The companies getting it wrong are the ones that see the demos and immediately give the agent admin access to everything.

**Jordan:** A compelling demo is not a security review.
