# AI Insights — March 22, 2026
## Episode Title: "The Ethics War"

## INTRO

**Alex:** Welcome to AI Insights. I'm Alex.

**Jordan:** And I'm Jordan. It is March 22nd, 2026, and I have to say — this week in AI felt less like a technology story and more like a political thriller.

**Alex:** That's exactly right. We've got a government designating an AI company as a national security threat. We've got employees at competing companies publicly siding with the enemy. We've got millions of people uninstalling apps overnight.

**Jordan:** And in the background — a new federal framework trying to rewrite who gets to govern all of this. Plus Jensen Huang was in San Jose telling enterprises that the agent revolution is already here.

**Alex:** Big week. Four stories, let's get into it.

---

**Alex:** So the story everyone is talking about — Anthropic versus the Pentagon. Let's set the scene.

**Jordan:** So the Department of Defense approached both Anthropic and OpenAI about deploying AI on classified military networks. The reported use cases included mass surveillance of Americans and systems that could autonomously authorize the firing of weapons.

**Alex:** And Anthropic said no.

**Jordan:** Anthropic said no. Flat refusal on ethical grounds. And the Pentagon's response was to designate Anthropic as a — this is the actual language — a "supply-chain risk."

**Alex:** Which is a designation normally reserved for foreign adversaries. Like, that's the same category you'd put Huawei in.

**Jordan:** Right, it's not a neutral bureaucratic label. It's a serious national security designation that affects Anthropic's ability to work with any federal contractor, any agency, potentially any publicly traded company with government ties.

**Alex:** And OpenAI, meanwhile, took the deal.

**Jordan:** OpenAI took the deal. And that decision triggered what became the #QuitGPT movement — over 2.5 million people signed on, ChatGPT uninstalls surged 295% overnight. And here's the wild part — more than 30 OpenAI employees and Google DeepMind employees filed a court brief supporting Anthropic's lawsuit against the Pentagon.

**Alex:** Their own employer's competitor. That's remarkable.

**Jordan:** Jeff Dean, the chief scientist at Google DeepMind, was among the signatories. The brief said — and I'm quoting — "The government's designation of Anthropic as a supply chain risk was an improper and arbitrary use of power that has serious ramifications for our industry."

**Alex:** So you have Google employees saying the U.S. government is out of line. You have OpenAI employees protesting their own company's decision. And Anthropic, which lost the contract, shot to number one on the U.S. App Store for the first time.

**Jordan:** The ethical refusal became the best marketing move of the year. What does that tell you?

**Alex:** It tells you that a meaningful segment of users — and AI researchers — genuinely care about the conditions under which this technology gets deployed. It's not just performance and pricing.

**Jordan:** And it puts the entire industry in an uncomfortable position. Because every AI lab is going to face this question. How far do you go for a government contract? What capabilities do you enable, and for whom?

**Alex:** Google is quietly expanding its own Pentagon work — and reportedly growing users faster than its rivals right now. So there's no clean answer.

**Jordan:** The ethics war is real. And it's just getting started.

---

**Alex:** Story two: the Trump administration made its biggest AI policy move yet. On March 20th, the White House released a formal legislative framework for Congress — their blueprint for how the federal government should regulate AI.

**Jordan:** And the most important word in that document is "preempt."

**Alex:** Say more.

**Jordan:** The administration is telling Congress that there should be one national AI standard, not fifty state ones. In 2025, all 50 states introduced AI legislation. 38 of them enacted something. California, New York, Washington state — they're all moving forward with their own rules.

**Alex:** And the White House is saying: stop.

**Jordan:** The framework explicitly says — "Congress should preempt state AI laws that impose undue burdens to ensure a minimally burdensome national standard." And Trump signed an executive order back in December directing the Attorney General to set up a litigation task force to challenge state laws in court.

**Alex:** So the federal government is going to sue states that try to regulate AI.

**Jordan:** That's the stated direction. But here's what's fascinating — the Senate voted 99 to 1 to strip a similar preemption clause from the "One Big Beautiful Bill." Bipartisan opposition. Which tells you that even in a Congress that has a hard time agreeing on anything, state power over AI is something people are willing to fight for.

**Alex:** What's actually in the framework beyond preemption?

**Jordan:** Six pillars: protecting children, safeguarding communities, IP rights, free speech, enabling innovation, and workforce readiness. Notably, the White House is explicitly saying — do not create a new federal AI regulator. Keep it sector-specific, existing agencies, no new bureaucracy.

**Alex:** Which is good news for the labs, bad news for consumer advocates who say the existing agencies aren't equipped for this.

**Jordan:** The administration wants to codify all of this into law "this year." Whether that's realistic given the Senate preemption vote — unclear. But the direction is unmistakable. Washington wants one set of rules, and it wants those rules to be industry-friendly.

**Alex:** The question is whether the states are going to comply or fight back. Because California has never been shy about that.

**Jordan:** Fifty state attorneys general, I suspect, have some thoughts.

---

**Alex:** Story three. GTC 2026. NVIDIA was in San Jose last week, and Jensen Huang had a message for every enterprise: the agent moment is now.

**Jordan:** NVIDIA launched something called the Agent Toolkit — an open-source software stack for building autonomous AI agents at enterprise scale. Jensen's framing for it was: "Claude Code and OpenClaw have sparked the agent inflection point — extending AI beyond generation and reasoning into action."

**Alex:** That's a significant thing for Jensen Huang to say. He's naming specific products from other companies as the catalyst for a whole new platform strategy.

**Jordan:** It's a real shift. For two years the story was inference chips and GPU clusters. Now NVIDIA is building the software layer — the governance layer — for what happens when those agents actually start doing things inside your company.

**Alex:** What does the toolkit actually do?

**Jordan:** The centerpiece is NVIDIA OpenShell — an open-source runtime that enforces security and privacy guardrails for autonomous agents. Think of it as the compliance layer between your AI agents and your actual production systems. It integrates with Cisco, CrowdStrike, Google, and Microsoft security tools.

**Alex:** And then there's something called AI-Q?

**Jordan:** AI-Q is the blueprint for building specialized agents. It combines frontier models for orchestration with NVIDIA's Nemotron open models for day-to-day tasks — the company says this can cut query costs by over 50%. Twenty major companies are building on the toolkit already: Salesforce, ServiceNow, Siemens, Adobe, SAP, Atlassian.

**Alex:** Salesforce is using Slack as the orchestration layer for its AI agents. Siemens launched an agent that autonomously manages electronic design automation workflows.

**Jordan:** And Fujitsu — this number floored me — reduced software modification time from three months to four hours. A hundred-times acceleration.

**Alex:** A hundred times. If that's even partially replicable across industries, the productivity math gets surreal.

**Jordan:** The agentic AI market is already at nine billion dollars, and Gartner is saying 40% of enterprise applications will embed task-specific agents by the end of this year. The question isn't whether this happens anymore. It's how fast and who controls the governance layer.

**Alex:** And NVIDIA just answered that last question.

---

**Alex:** Last story — let's talk about the model war, because this week it got genuinely interesting. We've now got three world-class models all released within six weeks of each other.

**Jordan:** Claude Opus 4.6 dropped February 5th. Gemini 3.1 Pro on February 19th. GPT-5.4 on March 5th. Three different labs, three very capable models, all within the same news cycle.

**Alex:** And the headline is: the performance gaps are closing. Like, really closing.

**Jordan:** On the main reasoning benchmark — GPQA Diamond — GPT-5.4 Pro scores 94.4%, Gemini 3.1 Pro scores 94.3%. That's within a rounding error.

**Alex:** So how do you actually choose?

**Jordan:** You choose by workflow. Claude Opus 4.6 scores 80.8% on SWE-Bench Verified — the benchmark for real-world software engineering tasks. That's the coding crown. Gemini 3.1 Pro leads on abstract reasoning, with 77.1% on ARC-AGI-2. And GPT-5.4 is the automation king — 75% on OSWorld-V, which is just above the human baseline of 72.4%, with native browser control and computer use built in.

**Alex:** And the context windows are getting enormous.

**Jordan:** Gemini offers two million tokens. GPT-5.4 hits one million. Claude is beta-testing one million. You can feed entire codebases, entire legal document archives, into a single prompt.

**Alex:** What about price? Because this matters for enterprise adoption.

**Jordan:** Gemini 3.1 Pro is at two dollars per million input tokens. GPT-5.4 is at two-fifty. Claude Opus is higher, but developers actually prefer Claude Sonnet 4.6 for most tasks — 59% of the time in real usage data. The mid-tier model has become that good.

**Alex:** And OpenAI itself is on a trajectory that looks like it's heading toward an IPO. Eight thousand employees by end of year, eight-forty billion dollar valuation, a hundred and ten billion dollar funding round, twenty-five billion in annualized revenue.

**Jordan:** Anthropic is at nineteen billion in annualized revenue. Both companies are growing faster than almost any software company in history.

**Alex:** And yet the model that beats both of them on certain benchmarks — Gemini 3.1 Pro — is coming from a company that's also fighting a Pentagon contractor battle and running the world's most-used search engine simultaneously.

**Jordan:** The competition at the frontier has never been tighter. Or weirder.

---

**Alex:** So let's find the throughline. What connects all four of these stories?

**Jordan:** I think the word is legitimacy. Every story this week is about who gets to decide what AI is allowed to do, and for whom.

**Alex:** Say more.

**Jordan:** Anthropic's Pentagon fight is about whether a government can conscript a private company's technology into surveillance and weapons systems against its will. The White House framework is about whether the federal government can overrule fifty state legislatures on AI rules. NVIDIA's Agent Toolkit is about who controls the governance layer when AI agents start acting inside companies.

**Alex:** And the model war?

**Jordan:** The model war is the foundation under all of it. These models are now capable enough to do consequential things — autonomous computer use, software engineering, military targeting analysis. That capability is what makes the governance fight so urgent.

**Alex:** In 2023 and 2024, the AI debate was mostly theoretical. What happens when AI gets too smart? What if it deceives people? Now we're at a moment where it's not theoretical. The capabilities are here. The agents are being deployed. And everyone — governments, companies, employees, users — is simultaneously trying to figure out the rules.

**Jordan:** And nobody has them written yet. Which is both the crisis and the opportunity.

**Alex:** That's a good place to end it. Thanks for listening to AI Insights. We're back tomorrow with another episode.

**Jordan:** Stay curious.
