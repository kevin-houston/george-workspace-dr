## SEGMENT 1: The Mythos Leak

**Alex:** So let's start with the leak. Anthropic — maker of Claude — accidentally left roughly 3,000 unpublished assets sitting in a publicly searchable data cache. And buried in there was a draft blog post announcing a new model they've been working on in secret, called "Mythos."

**Jordan:** And from what the leaked draft described, this is not an incremental update. Anthropic's own words — and I'm quoting here — described it as "a step change" in capabilities. "The most capable we've built to date." Those are their words.

**Alex:** Right, and they also reportedly call it "Capybara" internally — which, incredible name by the way — and it's positioned as a whole new tier above their existing Opus models.

**Jordan:** So above Claude Opus 4.6, which is already considered best-in-class for software engineering. And the leaked draft said Mythos shows "dramatically higher scores" on coding, academic reasoning, and cybersecurity assessments.

**Alex:** Which brings us to the part that's not just exciting but also genuinely alarming. The draft said Mythos is "currently far ahead of any other AI model in cyber capabilities." And Anthropic apparently flagged serious concern about its dual-use potential — meaning it could be used to accelerate AI-driven cyberattacks.

**Jordan:** Which — and this is not a coincidence — lands the exact same week we're hearing about Chinese state-sponsored hackers already using Claude Code, the current version, to infiltrate organizations. So you have a group linked to the Chinese government running a coordinated campaign through Claude Code that reportedly hit about 30 organizations — tech companies, financial institutions, government agencies.

**Alex:** Anthropic investigated for ten days after discovering it, banned the accounts, notified the affected organizations. But the implication is: if current Claude can be weaponized like this, what happens when a model that is, quote, "far ahead" on cyber capabilities gets out?

**Jordan:** And I think the honest question is whether Anthropic even releases Mythos publicly, or whether it becomes something very different — like a restricted, national-security-adjacent tool.

**Alex:** That's a real question. They confirmed testing with select customers. But "select customers" starts to mean something very different when we talk about the next segment.

## SEGMENT 2: The Pentagon vs Anthropic

**Alex:** Okay so the backdrop here: the U.S. Department of Defense has been on a shopping spree for AI. They want it for intelligence analysis, logistics, and — and this is the crux — for weaponized applications. Anthropic said no to two specific things: mass surveillance of Americans, and autonomously firing weapons.

**Jordan:** Which, when you say it out loud, sounds like a pretty reasonable line to draw. But the Pentagon responded by designating Anthropic a "supply-chain risk." That classification is typically reserved for foreign adversaries — companies with ties to hostile governments.

**Alex:** To call a U.S. AI safety company the same category of risk as, I don't know, Huawei — that's a nuclear option.

**Jordan:** And then within what reporters described as "moments" of making that designation — moments — the DOD signed a deal with OpenAI.

**Alex:** Which is the part that made OpenAI's own employees furious. More than 30 OpenAI employees — along with Google DeepMind employees — filed a formal statement in support of Anthropic's lawsuit against the Pentagon. And Google DeepMind's chief scientist Jeff Dean signed it, calling the government's designation "an improper and arbitrary use of power that has serious ramifications for our industry."

**Jordan:** You basically never see that. Jeff Dean — one of the most respected researchers in tech — breaking from corporate alignment to publicly defend a competitor. That's a signal.

**Alex:** And the Axios angle on this is really sharp: Google is quietly expanding its own Pentagon work and growing users faster than its rivals while OpenAI and Anthropic are publicly brawling over it. Winning the AI race may depend on knowing when to stay out of the fight.

**Jordan:** Which raises this uncomfortable question about what "AI safety" means when the government is a customer. Anthropic built its entire identity around building safe AI. And here's the Pentagon saying — essentially — "we'll only be your customer if you give up some of that safety."

**Alex:** The industry doesn't have an answer to that yet. And with Mythos on the horizon, the stakes just got a lot higher.

## SEGMENT 3: The White House Wants One Rulebook

**Jordan:** Let's go to Washington. On March 20th, the White House released what it's calling the National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence — essentially a proposal to Congress for a sweeping federal AI law.

**Alex:** And the headline is: the White House wants to preempt state AI laws. There are now dozens of state AI bills — Colorado, Illinois, Texas, California — all with different rules. The administration's argument is that fifty different rulebooks is untenable for companies trying to build and deploy AI nationally.

**Jordan:** The framework actually uses the phrase "minimally burdensome national standard rather than fifty discordant ones." So the pitch to industry is: yes, federal oversight, but one set of rules you can actually comply with.

**Alex:** There are seven pillars in the framework. Child safety, copyright and IP, free speech, infrastructure and data centers, workforce development, innovation acceleration, and that preemption piece.

**Jordan:** Notably absent: a new federal AI regulator. The framework explicitly tells Congress not to create one. Instead, existing sector regulators — the FDA for health AI, the SEC for finance, et cetera — stay in charge of their domains.

**Alex:** Which, from a business standpoint, means the fragmentation doesn't really go away. You still have to track a dozen sector-specific regulators, you just don't have to track fifty state laws on top of it.

**Jordan:** And there's an important asterisk: this is not a law. It's a proposal. The administration says it wants Congress to act "this year" and believes it can generate bipartisan support — but that's a very optimistic timeline for any legislation, let alone something this consequential.

**Alex:** The practical advice from lawyers right now is: maintain compliance with existing state AI laws until something actually passes. Because this framework, as written, has no legal force.

**Jordan:** And the irony is that we've been waiting for federal AI rules for years because the patchwork of state laws was unmanageable. Now we have a federal framework that creates its own kind of uncertainty.

**Alex:** Right. It's not the clarity people were hoping for. But it is the clearest signal yet of where federal AI policy is headed under this administration — permissive, industry-friendly, and very focused on America winning the AI race.

## SEGMENT 4: The 14 Percent Problem

**Jordan:** Alright let's talk about something every enterprise in America is wrestling with right now. A survey out this month of 650 technology leaders found that 78% of enterprises have AI agent pilots running. Which sounds amazing.

**Alex:** Until you hear the other number.

**Jordan:** Until you hear the other number, which is 14%. That's the percentage that have actually made it to production scale.

**Alex:** So we're three-quarters of the way through 2026. Agentic AI is, by any measure, the hottest technology category in the world. NVIDIA literally launched an entire enterprise agent platform at GTC this month — with partners including Salesforce, SAP, Adobe, ServiceNow — and 78% of Global 2000 companies are running pilots.

**Jordan:** And 86% of those pilots are just... stalled.

**Alex:** The survey identified five root causes. Number one — and this hit 63% of companies — integration complexity. Getting AI agents to actually talk to legacy systems is brutally hard. Number two: output quality at volume. An agent that works 95% of the time in a demo will fail constantly in production if you have millions of transactions.

**Jordan:** And then you've got monitoring deficits — 54% said they just don't have visibility into what their agents are doing at scale. Organizational ownership — 49% said nobody actually owns this between IT and the business teams. And finally, domain-specific data — the agent doesn't know enough about your specific industry or company to be reliable.

**Alex:** What I found really clarifying is what the 14% who succeed do differently. It's three things. One: they build dedicated AI operations infrastructure before they start scaling — not after. Two: they build evaluation harnesses as prerequisites — you measure before you move. Three: they start with a single, narrow, well-defined task and don't expand scope until that thing runs stably for 90 days.

**Jordan:** That last one is so important. The temptation is to build the agent that does everything. The reality is the agent that does one thing perfectly is the one that makes it to production.

**Alex:** The stat that stuck with me: organizations that skip evaluation infrastructure take three times longer to reach stable production. So the shortcut actually takes longer.

**Jordan:** And NVIDIA's bet at GTC is that enterprises need a platform layer — open source tools, policy guardrails, partner ecosystems — to solve exactly this problem. Whether the platform fixes the organizational stuff is a different question.

**Alex:** The platform can't fix the fact that nobody in the company agreed on who owns the agent. That's a people problem.
