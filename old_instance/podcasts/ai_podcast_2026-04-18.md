## SEGMENT 1: Snap's 65% Number — AI, Layoffs, and the New Math of Engineering Teams

**Alex:** So on April 15th, Snap announced it was laying off approximately 1,000 employees — about 16% of its total full-time workforce. The company also closed more than 300 open roles. That's a meaningful reduction.

**Jordan:** And on the surface you could read that as a struggling social media company cutting costs. But there's one number in the announcement that changes the entire framing.

**Alex:** AI now generates more than 65% of Snap's new code. The CEO, Evan Spiegel, said this directly in the announcement memo. He called it a "new way of working" and said the company would be smaller going forward by design.

**Jordan:** Which is different from saying layoffs were forced by bad financials. The stock jumped about 7% on the news. Investors are treating this as an efficiency story, not a distress story.

**Alex:** That distinction matters a lot. The layoffs are expected to reduce Snap's annualized cost base by more than $500 million by the second half of this year. Restructuring costs are $95 to $130 million in Q2. The math pencils out very favorably.

**Jordan:** But let's put this in context, because Snap isn't alone. Data from Layoffs.fyi shows more than 80 tech firms have cut over 71,000 jobs so far in 2026 — and AI automation is cited as a primary driver in most of them.

**Alex:** The 65% number is the one I keep coming back to. Most companies have been talking about AI-assisted development for two years. Snap is saying the majority of its new code is generated by AI — not assisted, not suggested, but generated. That's a qualitative shift.

**Jordan:** And the implication for engineering hiring is real. If you can go from a 6,000-person company to, say, 5,000 while maintaining or improving output, why wouldn't you? That's the question every CTO is asking right now.

**Alex:** There's a counterargument, which is that writing code was never the bottleneck at most engineering organizations. Architecture decisions, debugging production incidents, understanding domain requirements — those are still human jobs.

**Jordan:** For now. The honest answer is that the line keeps moving. Every time someone says "this part requires human judgment," a model comes along and does it adequately.

**Alex:** What I think is underappreciated is the compounding effect. Snap went from whatever that number was a year ago — maybe 20%, maybe 30% — to 65%. At what point does that curve bend toward 90%? And what does a 200-person engineering org with 90% AI-generated code look like?

**Jordan:** We're going to find out. That's the uncomfortable truth here. We are actively running that experiment in public.

## SEGMENT 2: Mythos — The Model Anthropic Built and Won't Let You Touch

**Alex:** Let's talk about the most technically remarkable story of the week, which is also one of the most ethically charged. Anthropic has been quietly running what they're calling Project Glasswing, which involves a model called Claude Mythos Preview. And what they found when they tested it is genuinely alarming.

**Jordan:** Mythos isn't a specialized security tool. It's a general-purpose language model — think of it as a more capable version of the Claude models we already use for writing and coding. But when Anthropic's engineers started testing its security capabilities, things got strange fast.

**Alex:** Specific facts here: Mythos fully autonomously identified and then exploited a 17-year-old remote code execution vulnerability in FreeBSD — one that gives anyone root access to a machine running NFS. That vulnerability existed in production systems for nearly two decades. Mythos found it and built a working exploit without being specifically prompted to look for that class of issue.

**Jordan:** And that's not an isolated case. According to Anthropic's disclosure, over the past several weeks, Mythos was used to identify thousands of zero-day vulnerabilities across every major operating system, every major web browser, and a range of other critical software infrastructure.

**Alex:** The thing that keeps me up about this is the democratization angle. Engineers at Anthropic — people with no formal security training — reportedly asked Mythos to find remote code execution vulnerabilities overnight. They woke up the next morning to a complete, working exploit.

**Jordan:** That is a capability that previously required nation-state resources or a very talented specialist. And now it's apparently something you can assign before you go to sleep.

**Alex:** So Anthropic's decision was: don't release this publicly. Instead, they gave preview access to a handpicked group of tech and cybersecurity organizations — Amazon, Apple, Broadcom, Cisco, CrowdStrike, the Linux Foundation, Microsoft, and Palo Alto Networks — under what they're calling Project Glasswing. The goal is to use Mythos to find and patch vulnerabilities before the model, or something like it, gets into the wrong hands.

**Jordan:** Anthropic is also committing up to $100 million in usage credits to this effort, plus $4 million in direct donations to open-source security organizations.

**Alex:** There's a real debate happening in the security community about the logic here. One side says: finding vulnerabilities isn't the hard part, patching them is. The infrastructure to fix identified CVEs hasn't scaled the way discovery has. So flooding the ecosystem with zero-day findings could actually increase risk in the short term.

**Jordan:** A Fortune piece this week quoted an industry veteran making exactly that argument — that the disclosure is moving faster than the remediation capacity.

**Alex:** The other side says: offensive capabilities like this exist whether Anthropic releases them or not. Nation-states already have comparable tools. Coordinating defense through a structured preview program is better than pretending the capability doesn't exist.

**Jordan:** What I think this story illustrates is something that's going to become a recurring theme. We're entering a period where the most capable frontier models aren't the ones you can actually access. There are more capable systems sitting in labs right now — Mythos is one confirmed example — and the decisions about who gets access to them are being made by a small number of people.

**Alex:** That's a governance question that doesn't have a clean answer yet.

## SEGMENT 3: Waiting for Spud — The GPT-6 Market Freeze

**Jordan:** Okay, let's talk about the model everyone is waiting for. OpenAI finished pre-training a model internally codenamed "Spud" on March 24th, at the Stargate data center in Abilene, Texas. Sam Altman told employees it was a "very strong model" that could "really accelerate the economy."

**Alex:** He said — quote — "a few weeks." That was March 24th. We are now April 18th. It has been 25 days.

**Jordan:** There was a widely circulated rumor that it would launch on April 14th. That date came and went. No blog post, no model card, no announcement.

**Alex:** And the standard window between pre-training completion and public release for OpenAI has been 3 to 6 weeks for safety evaluation and red-teaming. So the window is still open. Polymarket has it at 72% probability of launching by April 30th.

**Jordan:** Whether it ships as GPT-5.5 or GPT-6 hasn't been officially confirmed. OpenAI hasn't committed to either name publicly. What we do know is that Sam Altman has described it as the backbone of a unified super-app that brings together ChatGPT, Codex, and their browser product.

**Alex:** That's actually the more interesting product angle. It's not just a model upgrade — if the "super app" framing is accurate, it would be OpenAI making a move from API provider to destination platform. That's a different strategic posture.

**Jordan:** There's a market behavior element to this that I find fascinating. Enterprise procurement cycles for AI infrastructure are increasingly pausing ahead of expected major releases. Teams are waiting to see Spud's benchmarks before committing to annual contracts on the current generation.

**Alex:** Which creates this strange period of suspended animation. The models that exist right now — Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-5 Turbo — are genuinely excellent. And yet a meaningful segment of the market is essentially on hold.

**Jordan:** The developers I talk to describe it as trying to make architectural decisions when you know the ground is about to shift but you don't know how much.

**Alex:** The honest advice I've seen from a few engineering leads is: build against abstractions, not models. If your architecture assumes a specific model's behavior, you're building something fragile. If you can swap in a better model when it arrives, you're in good shape.

**Jordan:** The key question when Spud does drop — and it will — is whether it moves the capability frontier meaningfully or just incrementally. If it's what Altman's description suggests, we're going to see another wave of benchmark recalibration and product roadmap resets.

**Alex:** Given that the last four weeks have already had more major model launches than any previous month in history, that's a very consequential maybe.

## SEGMENT 4: Microsoft Agent Framework 1.0 — The Production Layer Arrives

**Jordan:** Let's close on something a little more infrastructure-y, because I think it deserves more attention than it's gotten in the last two weeks. On April 3rd, Microsoft shipped version 1.0 of its Agent Framework for .NET and Python.

**Alex:** And the headline here is the word "production." Microsoft described this as, quote, "the production-ready release: stable APIs, and a commitment to long-term support." That's not how they talk about experimental projects.

**Jordan:** What is this, exactly? For people who haven't been tracking it — Agent Framework 1.0 is a unified open-source SDK that Microsoft built by merging two existing projects: Semantic Kernel, which was their enterprise AI integration layer, and AutoGen, which is their multi-agent orchestration research framework.

**Alex:** By merging them, they've created something that covers the full stack: single-agent work, multi-agent orchestration, graph-based deterministic workflows, and enterprise memory management — all in a single SDK with stable APIs for both C# and Python.

**Jordan:** The model provider list is striking. Out of the box it supports Microsoft Foundry, Azure OpenAI, OpenAI, Anthropic Claude, Amazon Bedrock, Google Gemini, and Ollama. That's essentially the entire ecosystem.

**Alex:** The interoperability piece is the part builders should pay attention to. The framework supports both MCP — the Model Context Protocol that's now at roughly 97 million installs — and the A2A, or Agent-to-Agent, protocol. So your agents can call external tools over MCP servers, and they can also coordinate with agents running in entirely different frameworks.

**Jordan:** That second part is what moves this from "interesting SDK" to "infrastructure play." If A2A becomes the way agents communicate across systems, the team that controls the dominant A2A-compatible framework has a lot of leverage.

**Alex:** The enterprise features are also real. There's DevUI for visualizing agent execution in real time, OpenTelemetry integration for observability, checkpointing and pause/resume for long-running workflows, and human-in-the-loop approval hooks.

**Jordan:** The human-in-the-loop piece is underrated. One of the biggest complaints from enterprise deployments is that agentic systems go off the rails in ways you can't audit or interrupt. Built-in approval gates and checkpointing address that directly.

**Alex:** Gartner published its first standalone Hype Cycle for Agentic AI this month, placing AI agent development platforms at the Peak of Inflated Expectations. They also predict that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by end of 2027 due to cost overruns, unclear business value, or inadequate risk controls.

**Jordan:** That 40% cancellation stat is worth sitting with. The technology is ready. The question is whether organizations have the processes, the change management, and frankly the patience to deploy it properly.

**Alex:** Agent Framework 1.0 is a bet that those organizations need enterprise-grade infrastructure before they can answer that question. And Microsoft is positioning itself as the company that provides it.
