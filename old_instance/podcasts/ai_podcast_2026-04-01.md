## SEGMENT 1: GPT-5.4 Crosses the Human Baseline

**Alex:** So let's start with the story that honestly should be front page everywhere right now. OpenAI unveiled GPT-5.4 this week, and the headline number is this — on a benchmark called OSWorld-V, which simulates real desktop productivity tasks like navigating software, writing code, filling out forms, managing files — GPT-5.4 scored seventy-five percent. The human baseline is seventy-two point four percent.

**Jordan:** So the machine just passed the human.

**Alex:** On that benchmark, yes. And look, benchmark caveats apply — there's always debate about what these tests actually measure. But OSWorld-V is designed specifically to replicate the kind of computer use that an office worker does every day. It's not math olympiad problems or chess. It's "open this spreadsheet, find the anomaly, and fix it."

**Jordan:** And that's what makes it feel different. It's not beating humans at a game we designed to be hard. It's beating humans at the stuff we do at work.

**Alex:** Right. And paired with GPT-5.4's one-million-token context window — which means it can hold an entire codebase or a year of company emails in its working memory at once — you're starting to see what the "AI coworker" actually looks like in practice. Not a chatbot you type questions into. Something that can sit down at a computer and work an eight-hour day.

**Jordan:** I want to push on this a little, though. Because we've had benchmark moments before that felt like turning points and then kind of... didn't translate? GPT-4 was supposed to change everything in 2023. Then it turned out deploying it reliably in enterprises was really hard.

**Alex:** Totally fair. And Morgan Stanley actually just put out a warning about this — they're saying a transformative AI leap is coming in the first half of this year, but that most of the world isn't ready for it. Not because the tech won't work, but because the governance, the workflow integration, the change management — none of that has kept pace.

**Jordan:** So the machine is ready. The organizations aren't.

**Alex:** That's the gap. And we'll come back to that because it's the throughline for literally everything today.

## SEGMENT 2: Anthropic vs. The Pentagon

**Jordan:** Okay, next story, and this one is genuinely remarkable. The U.S. Department of Defense has officially labeled Anthropic — the company behind Claude — a supply-chain risk. That designation is normally reserved for foreign adversaries. Companies like Huawei. And the reason the DOD labeled them that way? Anthropic refused to let the military use its technology for two things: mass surveillance of Americans, and autonomously firing weapons.

**Alex:** Let that sink in for a second. An American AI company said "we won't build tools that spy on Americans or pull the trigger on weapons without a human in the loop" — and the Pentagon's response was to call them a national security risk.

**Jordan:** And almost immediately after making that designation, the DOD signed a new AI contract with OpenAI.

**Alex:** Which... is not a great look for OpenAI, by the way.

**Jordan:** No. And more than thirty OpenAI and Google DeepMind employees clearly felt the same way, because they signed a public statement defending Anthropic. These are employees of the company that just got the contract, saying that what the DOD did to Anthropic was wrong.

**Alex:** That's extraordinary. You don't often see employees at a direct competitor publicly defending the company that their own employer just financially benefited from. It tells you something about how seriously the AI safety community is taking this.

**Jordan:** It also tells you something about the fault lines that are forming inside these companies. There's a whole generation of AI researchers who went into this field because they cared deeply about getting it right — about safety, about human oversight. And now they're watching their employers navigate these massive government contracts and feeling real tension.

**Alex:** The core question this surfaces is one the industry has been dancing around: what are the limits? What should AI companies refuse to build, and who decides? Anthropic drew a line at autonomous weapons. The DOD didn't like it. And now we're all watching to see if that line holds or gets redrawn.

**Jordan:** It's worth pointing out that Anthropic has been the most vocal about putting safety principles in writing — their Constitutional AI approach, their published model spec. And the argument from the Pentagon might be that a company this important to national AI infrastructure shouldn't get to unilaterally decide what the military can and can't do with it.

**Alex:** Right. There's a real tension between "private company, private ethics" and "critical national infrastructure, public accountability." And we don't have a good framework for resolving that yet.

**Jordan:** We really don't. And that brings us directly to our next story.

## SEGMENT 3: The Federal vs. State AI Regulation War

**Alex:** So on March twentieth, the White House released something called the National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence. And the core argument is: one country, one set of AI rules. The federal government wants Congress to pass legislation that would preempt — meaning override — state AI laws that it deems "unduly burdensome."

**Jordan:** Which sounds reasonable until you look at the landscape of what's actually happening at the state level right now. Texas passed its Responsible AI Governance Act. Colorado has its AI Act. California has multiple bills taking effect this year around training data transparency and frontier AI oversight. These aren't fringe regulations. These are serious attempts by states to fill a void that the federal government left.

**Alex:** And the White House framework isn't just a policy suggestion. The administration directed the Attorney General to establish an AI Litigation Task Force — specifically to sue states whose AI laws it considers inconsistent with federal priorities.

**Jordan:** So they're not just saying "we'll make federal rules." They're saying "we will actively fight state rules in court."

**Alex:** Right. And California, per usual, is not backing down. Governor Newsom just signed an executive order pushing for stronger state-level AI oversight, directly defying the federal preemption push.

**Jordan:** What I find interesting about the White House framework is that it's not purely deregulatory. It actually has a child safety component, and provisions about intellectual property rights for AI-generated content, and it calls for standardizing energy use and permitting for AI data centers. That's a lot of actual regulation.

**Alex:** The difference is who's doing the regulating. The administration's view is that a patchwork of fifty state laws creates chaos for companies trying to deploy AI nationally. And there's a real argument there — if you're OpenAI or Anthropic, navigating fifty different bias audit requirements and disclosure rules is genuinely hard.

**Jordan:** But the counter-argument is that states have historically been the laboratories of democracy. California's car emissions standards became the national standard eventually. GDPR started in Europe and effectively became the global privacy baseline.

**Alex:** And if you strip away state authority before federal law is actually in place — which it isn't — you get a vacuum.

**Jordan:** Which might be exactly what some people want.

**Alex:** Michael Kratsios at the White House OSTP says they believe the framework can get bipartisan support. I'd love to know what timeline he's working from, because "bipartisan AI bill" has been promised for about four years now.

**Jordan:** To be generous — the AI Accountability Act did pass in March, requiring bias audits for AI in hiring, lending, healthcare, and criminal justice. So there is movement. It's just slower than the technology.

## SEGMENT 4: Tariffs Are Coming for Your Data Center

**Alex:** Okay, final story, and this is the one that I think gets the least attention relative to how consequential it is. America's AI buildout — and we're talking about a three trillion dollar projected spend over the next three years, per Morgan Stanley — is on a collision course with the Trump administration's tariff policy.

**Jordan:** Three trillion dollars. That number is almost too big to think about.

**Alex:** Right. Google, Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft alone spent over three hundred fifty billion on AI data centers in 2025. And now CSIS — the Center for Strategic and International Studies — has put out an analysis saying current and proposed tariffs could add seventy-five to a hundred billion dollars in additional AI infrastructure costs over five years.

**Jordan:** Which is equivalent to fifteen to twenty fewer hyperscale data centers. Those are the massive facilities that actually run the models we're talking about.

**Alex:** And the tariff exposure is specific. Semiconductors represent over fifty percent of AI server costs. Steel tariffs hit the physical infrastructure. There are fifty percent tariffs on grain-oriented electrical steel, which is what you need for the power systems that run these facilities. The average effective U.S. tariff rate jumped from about two and a half percent in late 2024 to around twenty-two percent by early last year.

**Jordan:** And this is happening right as American hyperscalers are trying to build the compute advantage that would keep the U.S. ahead of China in AI. It's a bit like trying to run a race while someone's tying your shoelaces together.

**Alex:** The CSIS report makes this point sharply — America's AI competitive advantage is in high-value services. Chip design, software, cloud infrastructure, AI consulting. Not manufacturing. So tariffs that raise the cost of building the infrastructure for those services are uniquely damaging to the thing that actually gives the U.S. its edge.

**Jordan:** And the irony is that the tariffs were designed in part to counter China's rise. But if they end up slowing American AI investment more than Chinese AI investment — which is largely insulated from U.S. tariffs because it's domestic — you've achieved the opposite of your goal.

**Alex:** There's also a distributional effect worth noting. Google and Microsoft can absorb cost increases. The companies that can't are the one thousand smaller AI labs that the CSIS report mentions — organizations with annual compute budgets under ten million dollars, which is already barely enough to train meaningful models. Those companies could get priced out of frontier AI entirely.

**Jordan:** So the big get bigger, the small get squeezed, and China keeps building. That's the scenario on the table.

**Alex:** Against that backdrop, the McKinsey Global Institute's 2026 update does note that AI spending has actually been the great offsetter of tariff damage globally — the boom in AI hardware trade between aligned economies has kept global trade growing even as traditional routes contracted. But that can't last forever if the infrastructure cost keeps rising.
